# Premium visual direction

Premium is a composition outcome, not a theme name. A dark background, a
gradient, blue-and-gold colors, many shapes, or the word "executive" does not
establish quality by itself.

## Start with a visual thesis

Before writing page code, freeze one sentence that describes the visual world
and why it fits the audience and subject. The sentence should name a material,
editorial, photographic, spatial, or diagrammatic idea rather than a software
UI style. Derive the palette, type scale, imagery, and recurring motif from
that thesis.

Examples of directions, not reusable templates:

- warm paper, carbon ink, and oxidized-metal details for an authoritative
  research briefing;
- near-black space, monochrome photography, and one mineral accent for a
  cinematic showcase;
- off-white editorial pages, hairline rules, and precision diagrams for a
  technical narrative.

Do not use "dark technology", "business blue", or "premium gradient" as the
entire rationale. Those labels do not constrain a design enough to guide it.

## Route consequence

- Use PPTMaster for higher-end commercial work when premium executive/client
  storytelling is the main value, content is sufficiently frozen, and strict
  preservation of an existing native PPTX is not needed.
- Use a task-specific native renderer as the default for other substantive
  audience-facing work. Write the deck around its actual narrative and reuse
  only stable mechanics such as typography, geometry, charts, and QA.
- Use `base-ppt` as the deliberate fast neutral route for simple, internal,
  recurring, or fixed-schema output. Do not select it merely because the deck
  needs editable native elements.
- Use an HTML/canvas route when packed visual fidelity matters more than native
  element editing, and disclose the editability trade-off.

A long deck, a technology topic, charts, or tables do not make a presentation
utilitarian. Route by the communication job and desired experience.

## Palette discipline

Use a dominant neutral canvas, clear text hierarchy, and either one restrained
accent or a compact semantic palette. Semantic colors must keep stable roles
and appear only where the evidence needs them. A Google-inspired system may use
blue for primary hierarchy, red for risk or contrast, contrast-safe yellow for
attention, and green for positive outcomes or action; blue should remain
dominant and the other colors sparse.

Avoid the familiar "technology dashboard" stack: navy plus neon cyan, violet,
gold, green, and red. Avoid alternating accent colors across otherwise
identical cards or decorative rainbow sequences. Metallic colors should behave like small material details,
not yellow fills. Blue is acceptable when it is justified; saturated royal
blue is not a universal signal of authority.

## Composition discipline

Storyboard at least the cover, one evidence page, the principal system or
architecture page, and the closing before implementation. They should not be
the same title-and-card skeleton.

- Build scale with typography, imagery, whitespace, and contrast, not with
  more chrome.
- Prefer one strong visual anchor to a field of dots, pills, glowing circles,
  badges, and rounded cards.
- Let evidence pages be quieter than hero pages. A dark cover does not require
  every content slide to be dark.
- Use diagrams, annotated comparisons, real charts, or relevant imagery where
  they explain the subject. A medium research deck made only of rectangles and
  text is a document inventory, not a premium presentation.
- Reuse alignment, type, and spacing for consistency; do not repeat a wide
  colored header rail or a dashboard grid on every page.

### Claim–proof–implication page anatomy

Most content pages should resolve into three levels that remain clear at normal
presentation distance:

1. A conclusion-led title states what changed or what the evidence means.
2. One proof object occupies roughly 55–70% of the useful body canvas. Prefer
   direct-label comparisons, real charts, causal flows, architecture maps,
   matrices, timelines, annotated exhibits, or evidence tables.
3. A short implication caption or conclusion rail answers “so what?” only when
   the proof does not already make it obvious.

Supporting text must stay subordinate to the proof object. Several equal cards,
even when neatly aligned, do not establish a focal point. For quantitative
comparisons, label values directly and make the comparison direction obvious;
avoid forcing the audience to decode a remote legend.

### Whole-deck rhythm

Use contrast as pacing, not as a universal skin. A distinctive cover and closing
may form strong bookends, with at most a small number of contrast section anchors
between them. Keep evidence pages quieter so charts and diagrams carry the
attention. Freeze a role-based palette, title/body/source type scale, and spacing
grid before implementation; do not choose new colors or font sizes slide by
slide. With no supplied reference, use at least 50pt for the deck title, 35pt
for slide titles, 24pt for subheads, and 16pt for ordinary body text, with
smaller type reserved for compact source/footer annotations.

## Premium QA questions

Visual inspection must be able to answer all of these from rendered slides:

1. Does the cover establish a distinct visual world instead of a generic
   technology theme?
2. Is the focal point obvious at normal viewing size?
3. Do the evidence and architecture pages use compositions suited to their
   claims rather than rearranged cards?
4. Are accent or semantic colors restrained and meaningful, with no decorative
   rainbow or neon-dashboard effect?
5. Does the closing resolve the opening with a synthesis or action?

If actual image inspection is unavailable, do not claim that these questions
passed.
